About the data

Map colors, year 0 to today

Each region takes the faith of the power ruling it in that year. Mixed shades show a conquest or conversion in progress. Colonial rule does not flip a color unless people converted. Officially atheist states show as black. Dates are approximate.

Modern Muslim share (red tint)

In Christian or black areas, the color is tinted toward red by the Muslim share of residents, starting from the first year that share was measured. A place at 40% Muslim is shaded 40% of the way to red.

  • England and Wales: 2011 and 2021 census, by council area (ONS via Nomis).
  • Scotland: 2022 census for Glasgow, Edinburgh, Aberdeen and Dundee; other council areas estimated from the national total. Northern Ireland: national census figure (0.2% in 2011, 0.6% in 2021).
  • United States: counties and states from the U.S. Religion Census, 2010 and 2020 (ARDA). The U.S. census does not ask about religion; these figures count mosque participants, so they are estimates. Counties under 5,000 people with unusually high figures are not tinted because they look like data errors.
  • Australia: New South Wales (2011, 2016, 2021 census) and Victoria (2021 census) from ABS QuickStats; the other states and territories share the 2021 average for the rest of the country, because QuickStats lists only the top five religions. Earlier years use the national figure.
  • Spain: 2019 shares by autonomous community from UCIDE, an unofficial count by Spain's Union of Islamic Communities (Spain's census does not ask about religion). Switzerland: 12 cantons, 2017, residents 15 and over (Federal Statistical Office); other cantons use the national figure. Before each regional figure, the national figure is shown.
  • Canada provinces: census 2001, 2011, 2021 (Statistics Canada, tabulated on Wikipedia).
  • All other countries: national Muslim share for 2010 and 2020 from Pew Research Center, applied evenly across the country.

Bars

Blue and red: before 2010, the share of the country's land ruled by Christian or Muslim powers on this map; from 2010, Pew's share of people. Green: mosques per 100,000 people, only where a count has been published, growing between counts. Hover a box for the counts and the number of Muslim national lawmakers.

  • U.S. mosques: 1,209 (2000), 2,106 (2010), 2,769 (2020), US Mosque Survey (source). Muslim members of Congress (source).
  • England and Wales registered mosques 1961 to 2011, and Muslim MPs 1997 to 2024 (source).
  • France 2,125 places of worship (2008) and about 2,500 (2016); Germany roughly 3,000 mosques (2016); Canada at least 458 mosques and Islamic centres (2024) (France, Germany, Canada).
  • No country publishes a count of Muslim local officials. Green pins mark well-documented city milestones instead.

Migrant fiscal balance

Net fiscal contribution per person aged 15+ per year, 2015 to 2019, for 26 EU countries: taxes and social contributions paid minus cash benefits and public services received (European Commission Joint Research Centre, 2020, Table A1). Circles show migrants born outside the EU; hover for natives and EU-born migrants. Results depend heavily on age, education and employment, and the study excludes spending on shared public goods such as defense.

Employment of migrants

Employment rate of people aged 20 to 64 by country of birth, yearly from 1995 where available (Eurostat, lfsa_ergacob), 36 European countries; the U.K. until 2019.

Happiness and misery scale

Happiness: life satisfaction on a 0 to 10 scale from the World Happiness Report (via Our World in Data). Misery index: inflation plus unemployment (World Bank). Working hours per worker: Penn World Table via Our World in Data. Life expectancy and income per person (PPP): World Bank. Each is shown on its own scale and compared side by side, not blended.

Birth rates

Children per woman and births per 1,000 people, yearly from 1950 (earlier for a few countries), from the UN World Population Prospects via Our World in Data. Replacement level is about 2.1 children per woman.

Hotspots: migration, protests, population changes

Population: World Bank World Development Indicators (total population, 1990 to 2025). Migrants born in low- and middle-income countries: UN DESA International Migrant Stock 2024 with World Bank income groups, via Our World in Data (1990 to 2024, every five years, straight lines between). England and Wales local areas: 2021 census country of birth (ONS via Nomis). Protest hotspots and counts use only the events in this atlas's protest list, located to a country by their map point. The comparison table shows rank correlations across countries; they describe how the numbers line up, not what causes what. U.S. county figures were not reachable from here.

Migration

All migrants: UN DESA International Migrant Stock 2024, via Our World in Data. Work migration: World Bank KNOMAD bilateral remittance estimates, 2021, and the ILO's 2022 migrant worker count.
